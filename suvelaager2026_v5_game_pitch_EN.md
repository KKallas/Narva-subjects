# Cyber Tag Arms — The Game

## The pitch

Two industrial robot arms. A glowing screen between them. Cyber threats popping up across the screen — short tails on the weak ones, long glowing tails on the high-value ones. You have **four physical tags** in your bin. Your opponent has four in theirs. The high-value threats expire in six seconds. You have to be **fast**, and you have to be willing to steal.

This is Cyber Tag Arms. It's a 90-second head-to-head game where two players control Dobot MG400 robotic arms. The scoring is brutally simple: every threat you neutralize banks you that threat's vector length. The team with more points wins. But you don't just race the clock — you race **each other**. And when your opponent places a tag on a juicy 25-point threat, you have five seconds to fly in, snatch their tag, carry it back to your bin, and steal the kill.

This is the simplest game in the camp and the most ruthless.

## What it feels like to play

You're sitting at one of two operator stations, controlling a Dobot MG400 on your side of the screen. Across from you, a member of the other team is doing the same thing in reverse. Between you, a 30-inch monitor pulses with neon shapes — angular threat silhouettes appearing at random positions, each with a glowing tail that tells you how juicy it is.

A short pink tail just appeared on your side. Worth maybe 8 points. Safe — it'll be active for 13 seconds. Plenty of time.

A long magenta tail just spawned dead center. **25 points.** It'll vanish in six seconds. You both saw it.

You go for it. Your arm grabs a tag from your bin, flies over the screen, drops it on the 25-pointer. Five-second countdown starts, your perimeter LED ring glowing red. You retreat your arm.

The other team's robot is already moving. Their gripper swoops in and **snatches your tag**. The ArUco flashes green. Now they're racing back to their bin with **your tag** in tow.

You have three seconds left on the countdown. They have to make it back to their bin before it expires, or your tag explodes mid-transit and you bank the points anyway.

You watch their arm. It's fast. It's almost back to their bin. Two seconds. One second. They drop the tag in their bin.

**Stolen.** 25 points to Green. The crowd at the table goes "ohhhhh."

You bite back. There's a 6-point pink threat right next to your bin. Easy points. You grab another tag, drop it, retreat. Countdown finishes uncontested. 6 points. Score is now 25-6 against you.

You decide to play dirty. Their robot is on the far side of the screen handling another tag. You fly over to **their bin** and steal one of their unused tags. You drag it back to your bin. You've now got 4 of yours plus 1 of theirs. They've got 2 left.

It cost you 4 seconds. Two threats expired unscored during that time. But now they're short on inventory.

The next 60 seconds are like this. Snatches, counter-snatches, bin raids, opportunistic placements. You start tracking patterns: the opponent always goes for high-value threats; the opponent always retreats to recharge after a snatch attempt. You change your placements accordingly.

The clock hits 5 seconds. There are two threats still on screen. You both have one tag left in your bin. You both go for the same threat. Your arm gets there first. **Place.** Five-second countdown.

The clock hits 0 while the tag is still counting. Match ends.

Final score: 187 to 173. You won. Three points. Their team gets 1 point.

You shake hands across the table. You both smile. They were better than you on snatches. You were better on bin denial.

## What it looks like as a spectator

Picture the tournament finals. Two teams of four at the table, one person operating each robot, three more shouting strategy. The screen between them is dark with neon shapes pulsing to a synth soundtrack. The score gap fluctuates wildly — 45 to 32, then 45 to 60 after a steal, then 78 to 60 after a quick double-neutralization.

The robots are moving fast — gripper rectangles in red and green flying across the screen, picking up tags, slamming them down on threats, snatching opponent tags mid-countdown. The thin dashed lines back to each robot base trace constantly-shifting geometry.

A long red tail appears at the corner. Worth 28 points. The Green robot lunges. The Red robot lunges. They both reach the threat at almost the same time, but Red drops their tag first. The Red countdown ring starts depleting.

The Green robot doesn't retreat — it stays right there. Two seconds in, it snatches the Red tag. The ArUco flashes green. Now Green is racing back to their bin with the snatched tag, and Green's strategist is shouting "GO GO GO." Red's team is screaming "BLOCK HIM."

Red's robot moves to physically intercept Green's path back to the bin. The Green operator panics, tries to go around. Three seconds left. Green's robot is almost back. Red's robot is in the way. The cameras tracking the ArUco markers register a collision-avoidance pause.

Green tries to go over. Two seconds. The Red robot moves up too. One second.

Countdown expires. The tag explodes mid-transit. The 28 points bank to **Red** because the snatcher didn't make it home. Red's team **screams.**

That's one moment. There are dozens like it across a 90-second match.

## The hook

This game works because every action has a counter, and every counter has a cost.

- **You placed a tag.** The opponent can snatch.
- **The opponent snatched.** You can chase or intercept.
- **You stole their tag from their bin.** You spent 4 seconds you could have used scoring.
- **You ignored their bin to focus on score.** They have all 4 tags and out-deploys you.
- **You went for the high-value threat.** It was contested — coin flip on who scores.
- **You ignored high-value threats for the safe 8-pointers.** You won't keep up with their score.

There is no dominant strategy. There is no "spam this" tactic. There is no "I'm winning by 30%, I can coast" — a single 30-point snatch can flip the lead in two seconds.

And every match ends in a clean outcome:
- **Win** — 3 points. Bragging rights.
- **Loss** — 1 point. You showed up. You played a real game.
- **Tie** — 2 points each. Wave it off, you'll get them next round.

No anticlimax. No "both lose because we couldn't cooperate." This is **head-to-head.** One arm vs the other. The screen knows who scored what.

## Why you'd want to be there

**If you're 14–16 years old:** you're playing what looks like a video game on the most expensive controller money can buy. Your industrial robot arm flies across a screen faster than your reflexes can react. You snatch. You get snatched. You bin-raid your friend's tag pool and watch them swear in real time. By the end of the day, you've played a tournament. You've operated the robot three different ways — first by joint sliders like a 1980s machinist, then by WASD like a normal game, then by **typing instructions** to an AI agent and watching it play for you. You've seen which mode wins which kind of match.

**If you're a parent or sponsor watching:** you see two industrial robots dancing around a screen with neon explosions, two operators with the kind of furrowed concentration you usually see in chess players, and three teammates per side screaming strategic call-outs to their operator. You see scores flipping in real time on the HUD. You see the team that didn't talk to each other lose to the team that did. You see a tournament bracket and finals.

**If you're a student deciding whether to come:** it's the simplest game in the camp, and the most competitive. It's the one you'll keep playing long after the tournament is over. It's also the one where your teammate's brain matters as much as your own — because the operator has to listen to the strategist, the strategist has to read the threats AND the opponent's intent, and the observer has to be tracking opponent patterns. One person can't win this.

This isn't a workshop. It's a sports match — built on real industrial robotics and real AI agents, themed as cybersecurity defense — that happens to be the most fun you'll have all summer.
