# Cyber Defense — The Game

## The pitch

Two industrial robot arms. A glowing screen between them. Five physical tools sitting in a tray above. A wall of cyber-threats rising from the bottom of the screen, glowing tails trailing behind them, accelerating toward an asset you have to protect. You have 90 seconds. You will probably lose. The question is **how well you lose, and who loses better.**

This is Cyber Defense. It's a tower defense game where you don't tap a touchscreen — you make a Dobot MG400 robot arm do it for you. Cooperation is mandatory. Competition is brutal. Every match ends in either mutual defeat or a points-split survival win. There is no draw, no anticlimax, no "well that was nice." You either held the line together or you watched the asset shatter while two robots stood there blinking.

---

## What it feels like to play

You're sitting at one of two operator stations, controlling a Dobot MG400 on your side of the screen. Across from you is another team's operator, controlling their robot. **You're both on the same team — you have to be.** The threats don't care who you are.

The threats spawn at the bottom. Small ones at first — short tails, slow speed. Easy to neutralize. Easy to ignore. You feel like maybe this won't be so hard.

Then the second wave hits. The tails get longer. The speeds get higher. Some of them aren't even coming straight at you — they're swerving in from diagonals you didn't expect. Your operator panics and grabs the Neutralize tool. The robot whirs, picks it up off the tray, places it on the screen near the closest threat. The LED ring around the tool starts counting down five seconds. The threat dies in a particle burst.

Now you have one tool down. Four left. Forever. They don't respawn.

A long-tailed threat is coming in fast on the left side. The other team's robot is closer. They place a Sandbox — freeze. The threat stops dead in its tracks for 20 seconds, glowing blue in suspended animation. The other team's operator pumps their fist. **Their** tool. **Their** score. You bite back.

You grab DDoS — the big one. The robot lifts it, sets it down in front of the asset, and a blue spiral pulls every threat in range toward the tool and holds them. You're feeding your team's score. You're also burning a tool that you can't get back unless someone clears it.

The other team grabs Honeypot. It's a 50/50. If it works, all the threats in range vanish in a particle burst — huge score for them. If it fails, those threats split into **twice as many**, smaller and faster, and now you're all in trouble.

It fails.

You have 45 seconds left. The screen is full of glowing tails. The asset is at 30% HP. Someone has to play Software Upgrade — the global defensive move that shortens every threat on the screen by 30%. It will save the team. It will give the user **zero score credit.** Someone has to take it.

The other team's operator hesitates. Yours doesn't. You eat the score hit. The threats shrink. The team survives a few seconds longer.

Whether you make it to 90 seconds is no longer the question. The question is whether you make it at all.

## What it looks like as a spectator

Picture this: it's the tournament round. Two teams are at the table, four people each. One person operating the robot. One yelling strategy. One watching the other team's screen for patterns. One typing furious updates to the AI agent's prompt.

The screen between the robots is dark with neon shapes pulsing to a synth soundtrack. **Geometry Dash aesthetic** — clean shapes, glowing tails, particle bursts, screen shake on critical hits. The threats are angular silhouettes with constant-length glowing tails — short tails for weak threats, long glowing tails for the brutal ones.

You see a wave of three long-tailed threats coming up the right side. Robot Green moves fast — picks up DDoS from the toolbox above the screen and slams it down in their path. The threats pull in. The blue spiral holds them. Robot Green retreats.

Robot Red moves to neutralize the leftover scout that slipped past. Picks up Neutralize. Wrong tool — but it'll do. Places it. Particle burst. Dead.

The team's strategist is shouting "BLUE TEAM IS SAVING SOFTWARE UPGRADE — DON'T WASTE YOURS." The team's prompter is updating the agent's instructions live: "Prioritize long-tail threats over short ones." The team's observer is tracking the other team's tool usage on a piece of paper.

The asset is at 12% HP with 8 seconds left.

The clock hits zero. The asset survived. The screen explodes in particle effects. The score appears: **Red team: 47 vector-units. Green team: 52 vector-units. Green wins 3 points. Red gets 1.**

Both teams scream. Both teams won, technically. One team won bigger.

That's a match. There are six of them across the tournament.

## The hook

This game works because every single mechanic has a counter, and every counter has a counter.

- **You placed a tool.** The other team can steal it before it fires.
- **The other team stole your tool.** You can pick up their spent tools afterward to reset them to the neutral pool.
- **You played the safe move.** The waves accelerated because the team is dominating. Now you're under pressure.
- **You played the risky move.** Honeypot failed and now the screen is overrun.
- **You played the selfless move.** Your team survived. Your score is zero.

There is no "spam the same thing" strategy. There is no "one player carries the team" strategy. There is no "I'm winning by 80%, I can coast" strategy. The waves adapt. The score is per-color. The selfless move costs you points. The team has to *talk* to win.

And every match ends in one of two clean ways:
- **The asset dies.** Both teams lose. The cyber-threats win. Silence at the table.
- **The asset survives.** One team gets 3 points. The other gets 1. Both walk away with something.

No draws. No mercy rules. No anticlimax. Either you held the line, or the line broke. Either you scored bigger than your neighbor, or you didn't.

## Why you'd want to be there

**If you're 14–16 years old:** you're sitting at a table with industrial robots that cost more than your parents' car, doing something that looks like the future. You're playing a game your friends back home can't play. You're getting credit for moves they wouldn't understand. By the end of the camp, you've operated a real robot arm three different ways — first by joint angles like a 1980s machinist, then by clicking like a 2000s CAD user, then by talking to an AI agent like a 2026 engineer. You've watched your prompts get smarter across the day. You've seen your team trust each other. You walk out with a tournament finish and a real understanding of why agent AI matters.

**If you're a parent or sponsor watching:** you see a room full of teenagers screaming strategy at each other, robots whirring, neon lights pulsing, scores updating in real time. You see them argue about whether to play Software Upgrade. You see them point at each other across the table. You see them shake hands at the end. You see a tournament bracket on the wall and finalists. You don't see anyone on their phones. You see what the camp delivered.

**If you're a student deciding whether to come:** it's two days. You'll lose more matches than you win. You'll eat the points hit by playing the selfless move at least once. You'll watch your team's prompt get rewritten between rounds and feel it click. You'll be on Instagram about it for a week. You'll remember it for years.

This isn't a workshop. It's a game. And it's a serious game — built on real industrial robotics, real AI agents, real cybersecurity narrative — that happens to be the most fun you'll have all summer.
